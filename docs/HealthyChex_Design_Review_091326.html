Interface audit · iOS

HealthyChex Design Review

Ten prioritised changes, ranked by what they cost the person filling in the form — an adult somewhere between 45 and 85, often at a bedside or a kitchen table, answering questions about their own body.

v4.1.1 · Sep 2026Measured at 430×932 (Pro Max)58 recommendation types

The engineering is ahead of the interface

Contrast, touch targets, focus states and reduced-motion are all handled — that work is genuinely done, and most apps never get there. The recommendation engine is validated at 5,800/5,800. So this review spends no time on fundamentals that already pass.

The defining problem is type size, and it is not a matter of taste. This app begins screening at 45. Presbyopia — the loss of near focus that sends people to reading glasses — also begins around 45 and is effectively universal by 55. The app's entire user base is the population least able to read 11px text, and the results screen goes down to 8px. Findings 1 and 2 follow from that single fact and are worth more than the other eight combined.

Already solid

  • ✓Contrast — every sampled pair passes AA; body text at 8.99:1
  • ✓Touch targets — zero controls under 44×44pt
  • ✓Focus + motion — :focus-visible and prefers-reduced-motion both honoured
  • ✓Icon buttons — all carry accessible labels

Needs work

  • ✕Type scale — 2 steps below iOS HIG throughout
  • ✕Dynamic Type — 207 px-based declarations, no response to system text size
  • ✕Dark mode — no prefers-color-scheme block
  • ✕Form semantics — no <label for>, no autocomplete, wrong keyboard for age
01

Raise the type scale to iOS HIG

Critical

Every token sits roughly two steps below Apple's scale. What the app calls body is Apple's subhead; what it calls callout is Apple's footnote. The result is a clinical form whose questions are set smaller than the caption under a photo.

HealthyChex display 22 · title 17 · body 15 · callout 13 · foot 12 · cap 11
iOS HIG title2 22 · headline 17 · body 17 · callout 16 · foot 13 · cap 12
Results view contains 8px and 9px text — below the 11pt floor

Shift the whole ramp up: body 17, callout 16, foot 13, caption 12, and set an absolute floor of 11 — nothing in the results view below it. Because every size already comes from --f-* tokens, this is a six-line change, then a re-run of your fit measurements. Expect a step or two to overflow again; that is the real cost of this finding, and it is worth paying.

02

Support Dynamic Type

Critical

A patient who has already turned up system text size in Settings — the single most-used accessibility feature on iOS, and near-ubiquitous past 60 — sees no change whatsoever in your app. Every size is a hard pixel.

Font declarations 207 in px · 40 in rem

Redefine the --f-* tokens in rem so they inherit the user's root size, and let the frame breathe rather than clamping it:

:root{
  --f-body:1.0625rem;   /* 17px at default */
  --f-callout:1rem;     /* 16px */
  --f-foot:0.8125rem;   /* 13px */
  --f-cap:0.75rem;      /* 12px */
}
html{ -webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; }

Then re-test at 200% text size. Steps will overflow — which is exactly why the scroll cue you just added matters, and why input steps should degrade to scrolling gracefully rather than being pinned to one screen at any cost.

03

Ship a dark theme

High

There is no prefers-color-scheme block anywhere in the file. A hospitalist checking something at 3am, or any patient whose phone is in dark mode all day, gets a full-brightness white sheet. The groundwork is already laid — the design tokens exist and the page ground is already a deep green-black.

Add a token-only dark block. Do not invert: keep the teal accent readable by lifting it (#0C6B54 → roughly #4FBF9B) and let the card surfaces sit just above the ground rather than pure black. Roughly 20 token overrides, no component changes.

04

Drop the phone bezel on a phone

High

The simulated iPhone frame is lovely on a desktop preview and slightly absurd on an actual iPhone — a phone drawn inside a phone. It costs a rounded mask that clips content near the corners, plus vertical space you have spent this whole session fighting to reclaim.

Keep the bezel as a desktop affordance only, and go edge-to-edge on device:

@media (max-width:500px){
  .iphone{ border-radius:0; box-shadow:none; }
  .screen{ height:calc(100dvh - env(safe-area-inset-bottom)); }
  body{ padding:0; }
}

Use env(safe-area-inset-*) rather than fixed offsets so the home indicator and the Dynamic Island are handled by the system instead of by your arithmetic.

05

Fix the form semantics

High

Three separate problems in the first step. Age is type="number" with no inputmode, so iOS offers the full keyboard instead of a number pad. The name fields have no autocomplete, so nothing autofills. And none of the three has a real <label for> — the visible text is a <span>, so VoiceOver announces only the placeholder, which disappears the moment typing starts.

#ag type=number · inputmode none · label none
#fn/#ln autocomplete none · label none

Use real labels tied by for, add autocomplete="given-name" / "family-name", and give age inputmode="numeric". Worth noting your smoking fields already do this correctly — inputmode="numeric" and a proper label — so the pattern exists in the file; it just needs applying to step 1.

06

Give "unanswered" a visible state

High

Now that nothing is pre-selected — the right call — an unanswered question and a question the person deliberately skipped past look identical. The only signal is the footer hint, which names what is missing but doesn't point at it. On a step with three questions, the person has to map a list of names back onto rows.

Mark the row itself. A 2px dashed left rail in your rust tint on any .irow whose value is still empty, cleared the moment it's answered, turns the hint into a confirmation rather than a lookup. Pair it with aria-live="polite" on the hint so VoiceOver announces what remains as it changes.

07

Stop the progress bar moving backwards

Medium

The denominator changes mid-flow. Answering "Yes" to family cancer history takes the count from 6 steps to 7, and Yes to genetic syndrome takes it to 8 — so the bar can visibly retreat as a reward for giving more information. The count is honest, but the fill reads as lost ground.

Two clean options. Either keep the proportional fill and compute the denominator from the maximum possible path so it only ever shrinks the remaining distance, or drop the proportional fill on this screen and show discrete pips — one per step — which handles a changing count without implying regression.

08

Let the results card lead with the action

Medium

Each recommendation currently carries the guideline body, a grade badge, a frequency pill, a status control and a reference superscript at near-equal weight, much of it at 8–9px. The one thing the person came for — what do I do, and when — competes with its own provenance.

Set a clear primary line per card (the action and the interval, at body size), keep the status control prominent because it's the interactive part, and demote the grade badge and citation into a tap-to-expand row. You already have the disclosure pattern working in the grade popovers — reuse it. The evidence stays one tap away, which is the right distance for a patient and no real cost to a clinician.

09

Make step transitions directional

Medium

Forward and back currently use the same fade, so the interface gives no spatial feedback about which way the person moved. Across eight steps plus edit-jumps from the review screen, that's the difference between a flow someone holds a mental model of and one they merely endure.

Slide the incoming step in from the right on Next and from the left on Back, ~200ms, with the existing prefers-reduced-motion guard already in the file collapsing it to a plain swap. An edit jump from the review screen should use the Back direction, then return forward — that's what makes "go fix that one answer" feel like a round trip rather than a detour.

10

Clean up two pieces of stray chrome

Medium

The brand bar reads "iPhone 17 preview" — developer scaffolding sitting in the most prominent horizontal strip on the screen, telling the person nothing about their own health. And the medical disclaimer now appears only on the review step, which I moved there to buy vertical space; for a tool that generates clinical recommendations, that trade deserves your explicit sign-off rather than mine.

Replace the preview label with the active profile name — it's the one piece of context that earns that position in a multi-profile app — or drop the strip entirely on device. For the disclaimer, consider a persistent one-line footnote in the results view, where recommendations are actually read, rather than only on the screen before they're generated.

If you only do three

  1. Findings 1 and 2 together. They are the same fix approached twice — get the scale right, then make it respond to the system. Everything else is refinement on top of a form your users can read.
  2. Finding 5. An afternoon's work, and it makes the first screen — the one with the highest abandonment risk in any form — autofill, open the right keyboard, and read correctly under VoiceOver.
  3. Finding 6. It completes the change you just made. Removing the defaults was the clinically correct decision; showing people what's still blank is what makes it feel like care rather than an obstacle.
